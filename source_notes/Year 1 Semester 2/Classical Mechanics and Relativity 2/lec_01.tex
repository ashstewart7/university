\section{Rotational Mechanics}
We've been introduced to a number of equations for translational mechanics such as:
\[
    F = ma
\]
\[
    W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}
\]
\[
    K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
\]

In simple cases that we'll deal with on this course, these all have equivalent formulae for rotational mechanics. Instead of position $r$, we have angle $\theta$. Instead of velocity $v$, we have angular velocity $\omega$. Instead of acceleration $a$, we have angular acceleration $\alpha$. Instead of mass $m$, we have moment of inertia $I$. Instead of force $F$, we have torque $\tau$. Etc\dots

The main goal is to discover alternative formulae for rotational quantities, and to understand how they relate to the translational ones.

\section{Centre of Mass}
We can consider two possible cases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A single point like object, such as a particle.
    \item An extended object, such as a rod or a disc, made up of many particles.
\end{enumerate}

Given a system of $i$ discrete masses, $m_i$, located at positions $\vec{r}_i$, the centre of mass $\vec{R}$ is given by:
\[
    M \vec{R} = \sum_i m_i \vec{r}_i    
\]  
Where $M = \sum_i m_i$ is the total mass. For every component mass $m_i$, we consider two types of net forces acting upon it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Internal forces, $\vec{f}_i^\text{int}$.
    \item External forces, $\vec{f}_i^\text{ext}$.
\end{itemize}

The internal forces are those exerted by other masses in the system, and the external forces are those exerted by objects outside the system. Hence:
\[
\vec{f}_i^\text{int} = \sum_{j \neq i} \vec{f}_{ij}
\]

We want to consider how an object moves, and if we assume the object has a constant mass and is rigid (does not deform) then we can consider the motion of the centre of mass.
\[
M \ddot{\vec{R}} = \sum_i m_i \ddot{\vec{r}}_i = \sum_i m_i \vec{a}_i = \sum_i \left(\vec{f}_i^\text{int} + \vec{f}_i^\text{ext}\right)
\]

\[
= \sum_i \left( \sum_{j \neq i} \vec{f}_{ij} + \vec{f}_i^\text{ext} \right)
\]

\[
= \sum_i \sum_{j \neq i} \vec{f}_{ij} + \sum_i \vec{f}_i^\text{ext}
\]

However, if one internal mass exerts a force on another, then the second mass exerts an equal and opposite force on the first. Hence, the internal forces all nicely cancel out:
\[
\sum_i \sum_{j \neq i} \vec{f}_{ij} = 0
\]

\[
M \vec{\ddot{R}} = \sum_i \vec{f}_i^\text{ext} = \vec{F}^\text{ext}
\]
This is the analogue of Newton's 2nd Law but for a non-point-like object. For a rigid body, the centre of mass moves as if the entirety of the object's mass were concentrated at that point and all external forces were applied at that point.

For a rigid body, we can break down the motion into two components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Translational motion of the centre of mass.
    \item Rotational motion about the centre of mass (in the centre of mass frame)
\end{itemize}

\section{Continuous Objects}
However, many extended objects can be thought of as having a continuous distribution of mass, rather than being made up of finite discrete masses. 

We define the density $\rho(\vec{r})$, such that $\rho(\vec{r}) dV$ is the mass contained in an infinitesimal volume element $dV$ around the point $\vec{r}$. While a point is massless, a small volume can contain a mass, albeit a tiny mass element. We can therefore get the total mass by integrating across all of these mass elements (effectively, we just swap sums for integrals).

Note that:
\[
    dV = dx \, dy \, dz \equiv d^3 \vec{r}
\]
